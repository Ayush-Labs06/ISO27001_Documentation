# IRL Tips — Management Review

---

## 1. CEO Attendance Is Non-Negotiable — and Auditors Know When It's Fake

ISO 27001 Clause 9.3 explicitly requires top management (CEO/executive leadership) to conduct the review — not just the ISMS Owner presenting to themselves. Auditors will check the attendance list. A management review attended only by the ISMS Owner is a nonconformity.

In practice: put the management review on the CEO's calendar at the start of the year. Call it a "security board meeting" if that's the language that gets attendance. The 90 minutes is worth it — it's the one time a year where the CEO has to engage with IS risk, which is exactly what the standard is trying to achieve.

If the CEO is truly unavailable (holiday, illness): reschedule. Don't hold the review without them and then add their name to the minutes.

---

## 2. "Decisions and Actions" Is the Most Important Output

Many management reviews produce a well-formatted minutes document that looks good but contains no actual decisions. "The team discussed the risk register and found it to be adequate" is not an output — it's a description of a conversation.

ISO 9.3.3 requires the review to produce:
- **Decisions** (yes/no choices made by management)
- **Actions** (with named owners and due dates)
- **Resources** (if more budget or headcount is needed)

Force the meeting to produce at least 3–5 concrete actions. If management reviews your ISMS and genuinely has nothing to decide or change — that might be a good sign, or it might mean the review isn't surfacing real issues.

---

## 3. The 9.3.2 Inputs Checklist Is Your Legal Requirement, Not a Suggestion

The standard lists specific mandatory inputs in Clause 9.3.2(a)–(e). Certification body auditors will read your management review minutes and check each input is present. A common minor NC: "Management review minutes do not address changes to interested parties' requirements (9.3.2(b))."

Use the inputs checklist in this folder as a pre-flight before every review. If you can't tick every box, you have a gap to address before the meeting — not after.

---

## 4. The KPI Dashboard Is What Makes the Review Substantive

Without metrics, a management review is just a meeting. The KPI dashboard is what allows the CEO to make informed decisions. Common problem: ISMS Owner shows up to the review saying "things are generally OK, we had a couple of incidents" with no numbers.

Build the dashboard monthly — not the week before the review. Month-over-month trends are far more valuable than a single snapshot. A phishing click rate that went from 18% → 15% → 12% → 9% over 4 quarters is a story worth telling. A single 9% data point means nothing.

---

## 5. Auditors Will Check That Actions from Last Review Were Completed

The single most common management review failure: actions from the previous review that were never tracked or completed. If your review minutes say "Action: DevOps Lead to implement GuardDuty findings review process by [date]" and the next review has no follow-up — that's a minor NC and evidence of a failing management system.

Build action tracking into the format: every review opens with "What happened to the actions from last time?" with a live status. Actions that are routinely incomplete signal to auditors that management review is treated as a compliance exercise, not a genuine governance activity.

---

## 6. Annual Is the Minimum — Semi-Annual Is the Reality for Pre-Cert

For clients aiming for certification within 12 months, one management review is barely enough. The first review typically surfaces major gaps, produces many actions, and is somewhat chaotic. The second review is where management demonstrates they acted on those outputs.

Recommend two reviews in year 1: one at roughly the mid-point of implementation (6 months in) and one 4–6 weeks before Stage 2. The pre-Stage-2 review lets management endorse the certification readiness decision — and gives you audit evidence that top management was engaged right up to the audit.
